# Back pain therapy in Chatsworth that finds the cause, not just the sore spot

About 60 minutes, one-on-one with Dr. Ed, every visit, and a home plan that keeps the relief going between sessions.

### Who back pain therapy is for

Back pain brings more people to Chatsworth Physical Therapy than anything else. Most of them arrive in one of these situations.

- **It has been weeks and it is not going away.** Lower back pain that is now part of every morning, every car ride and every night, and the stretches from the internet have not touched it.
- **It flared and you can barely move.** You went to pick something up, or you simply stood up, and now getting in and out of the car or bed is a project. (One patient came in exactly like this and was moving comfortably by the end of the week; his review is below.)
- **It runs down your leg.** Sciatica, a pinched nerve or a disc problem: pain, tingling or numbness into the buttock, thigh or foot, with or without back pain.
- **You have tried other things.** Massage, a chiropractor, another physical therapist, and the relief never lasted more than a day or two.
- **Your work or your sport keeps setting it off.** Desk posture, driving, lifting, or a sport like gymnastics or golf that loads the spine the same way over and over.

### What treatment looks like with Dr. Ed

The first visit is an evaluation of about 60 minutes, depending on what you need, one-on-one with Dr. Ed. He does not start with the sore spot. He watches how you stand, bend, twist, sit and walk, tests the hips, the mid-back and the muscles that are supposed to be holding the spine steady, and checks whether a nerve is involved. Back pain usually has a cause somewhere other than where it hurts, and the evaluation is where he finds it. Then he explains it in plain terms and tells you what the plan is.

Every visit after that is about 60 minutes with Dr. Ed, nobody else, and usually mixes:

- Hands-on manual therapy: joint mobilization and soft-tissue work to loosen what is stiff and calm what is irritated. Patients describe it as limited but very effective.
- Targeted strengthening for the deep core, the hips and the glutes, so the spine stops doing all the work.
- Posture and movement correction: how you sit at the desk, lift, get out of the car, sleep.
- Therapeutic ultrasound, electrical stimulation and red light therapy where they speed tissue recovery or settle a muscle spasm.
- A short written home program, a few exercises you will actually do, adjusted every visit. Patients say the home tips are what keep the pain manageable between visits.

Dr. Ed does not just treat symptoms; the aim is to solve the underlying issue causing your back pain so it stops coming back. How many visits that takes depends on what the evaluation finds, so it is set after the first visit, not quoted up front. Evening and Saturday appointments are open until 8pm.

### Common questions about back pain therapy

**Can I come in while my back is still in spasm?**
Yes, and it is a good time to come. Dr. Ed adjusts the first visit to what you can tolerate, and the goal of that visit is to get you moving more comfortably, not to put you through a workout.

**Do I need an MRI or X-ray first?**
No. Most back pain is diagnosed by how you move and what reproduces the pain, which is what the evaluation does. If imaging is needed, Dr. Ed will say so. If you already have it, bring the report.

**How many visits will it take?**
It depends on what is causing the pain and how long it has been there. Some patients feel a clear difference after the first visit; longer-standing problems take longer. Dr. Ed sets the plan after the evaluation and tells you if he thinks it will be short.

**Will the treatment hurt?**
Some hands-on work is uncomfortable for a moment, and you will be told before it happens. Nothing is forced, and you can stop anything at any time.

**Can physical therapy help sciatica?**
In most cases, yes. Treatment aims at what is irritating the nerve, usually a disc or a tight muscle in the hip, and at the movements that keep irritating it.

**Do I need a referral?**
No referral is needed to start. California law lets you see a physical therapist without a referral for up to 12 visits or 45 days, whichever comes first. After that, we need a referral from your doctor to continue. Medicare patients need a referral from the start.

# Sports physical therapy in Chatsworth, one-on-one with Dr. Ed

Rehab for the injury, strength for the weak link, and a step-by-step return to your sport instead of a blanket "rest".

### Who sports physical therapy is for

Athletes of every level come to Dr. Ed, from a 10-year-old gymnast to a weekend runner. The situations are usually these.

- **A fresh injury.** A rolled ankle, a pulled hamstring, a knee that gave out, a shoulder that will not lift. You want to know what it is, how bad it is and when you can play.
- **A pain that will not clear.** Shin splints, tennis elbow, runner's knee, a sore Achilles, a back that hurts on every backbend. It has been weeks and it flares every session.
- **After surgery.** ACL reconstruction, a rotator cuff repair, a meniscus repair, and you want rehab that treats you like an athlete going back to competition, not a patient going back to the couch. (Full detail on the [post-op physical therapy](/pre-post-surgical-rehabilitation/) page.)
- **A young athlete.** Gymnasts, dancers and team-sport kids whose growing bodies get hurt differently; see [pediatric physical therapy](/pediatric-physical-therapy/).
- **Injury prevention.** A history of ankle sprains or an ACL in one knee, and you want the other side protected before the season.

### What treatment looks like with Dr. Ed

The first visit is an evaluation of about 60 minutes, depending on what you need, one-on-one with Dr. Ed. He looks at the injured area and at everything above and below it: how you land, cut, squat, throw or reach, where the strength is missing and which movement keeps reloading the injury. He explains what he found and what the plan is, including a realistic line on when you can train and when you can compete.

Every visit after that is about 60 minutes with Dr. Ed, nobody else, and usually mixes:

- Hands-on manual therapy for the injured tissue and the joints around it.
- Strength and control work for the muscles that should have protected it, built up week by week.
- Mobility and flexibility work where the sport needs it, especially for gymnasts and dancers.
- Sport-specific drills: landing mechanics, cutting, sprinting, throwing, whatever your sport asks for, added as you earn them.
- Electrical stimulation, therapeutic ultrasound, red light therapy and kinesiology taping where they help recovery or let you train sooner.
- A written home program and a note for your coach on what is and is not allowed this week.

Dr. Ed schedules around training. Appointments run until 8pm Monday to Friday and on Saturdays, and patients say he fits sessions around busy schedules, late at night or during the day. How many visits you need depends on the injury; he sets it after the evaluation.

### Return to sport, step by step

Pain going away is not the same as being ready to play. The injury that comes back is usually the one that was rested, felt fine and was never retested. Dr. Ed runs rehab in stages: calm the injury, restore range, rebuild strength, then retest you against what the sport demands (single-leg hop, cutting, overhead load, backbends) before you go full speed. You know where you are at every visit and what the next gate is.

For athletes who have had an ACL injury or repeated ankle sprains, the same testing becomes a prevention program for the other side.

# Post-op physical therapy in Chatsworth, one-on-one with Dr. Ed

Your surgeon's protocol, followed one-on-one for about 60 minutes a visit, with a home program that keeps the recovery moving between sessions.

### Who post-op physical therapy is for

- **After a joint replacement.** A new knee or hip, discharged with a sheet of exercises and a prescription for outpatient physical therapy, and you want someone who will actually get you walking normally again.
- **After an orthopedic repair.** ACL reconstruction, rotator cuff repair, meniscus repair, labral repair, a fracture that was plated or pinned.
- **After spine surgery.** A discectomy, a fusion or a decompression, with nerve symptoms or weakness that need careful, staged work.
- **Before surgery.** Prehab: getting the joint as strong and mobile as possible before the operation, which makes the recovery after it faster. Some patients find that a few weeks of therapy before surgery changes whether they need it at all; that is a conversation between you and your surgeon, and Dr. Ed will give you an honest read.

### What treatment looks like with Dr. Ed

Bring your surgeon's post-op protocol and your discharge paperwork to the first visit. Dr. Ed works from your surgeon's instructions, not a generic plan, and the evaluation, about 60 minutes depending on what you need, sets your starting point: swelling, range of motion, strength, how you walk or reach, and what you need to do again at home and at work.

Every visit after that is about 60 minutes with Dr. Ed, nobody else, in the stages your surgery needs:

- Early: controlling swelling and pain, protecting the repair, gentle range of motion, and getting you safe on stairs and in the car. Therapeutic ultrasound, electrical stimulation and red light therapy are used here where they help, and patients say they made a real difference to recovery after ACL and shoulder surgery.
- Middle: restoring full range of motion with hands-on work, waking the muscles back up, rebuilding walking or reaching so it looks normal again.
- Late: strength, balance and the specific things you want back, whether that is gardening, hiking, lifting a grandchild or returning to sport (see [sports injury recovery](/sports-injury-recovery/)).

You leave every visit with a written home program, adjusted as the repair heals. A family member is welcome to sit in. The number of visits depends on the surgery and your surgeon's timeline; Dr. Ed sets it after the evaluation. Evening and Saturday appointments mean a working family member can drive you.

### The three recoveries we see most

**Knee replacement.** The first six weeks decide the range of motion you will have for years. Getting the knee to bend and straighten fully, early, is the work, and it is hands-on, patient work. Patients who do the home program between visits get there faster.

**Hip replacement.** Walking without a limp, restoring hip strength and rebuilding balance so you trust the new joint. One patient after hip replacement wrote that Dr. Ed was intuitive about finding ways to fix her balance and strength; her full review is on the [reviews page](/reviews/).

**ACL reconstruction.** A nine-to-twelve-month road for an athlete, done in stages and tested before each one. The patient whose review is below did his rehab here and said it was extremely beneficial on the road to recovery.

Red light therapy puts low-level light on the treated area. Researchers call this kind of treatment photobiomodulation, and many studies use the name low-level laser therapy.

The research is mixed. [A 2017 review in the European Journal of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145397/) pooled earlier studies and found that adults with muscle and joint problems had lower pain on average with low-level laser therapy than in comparison groups. Results differed widely from study to study.

[A 2018 assessment by the Washington State Department of Labor and Industries](https://www.lni.wa.gov/patient-care/_docs/HTAupdateLLLT_011818.pdf) rated the overall quality of the evidence as low or very low and called the results on whether it works highly inconsistent. It saw a small short-term difference from placebo for some conditions, and said it was uncertain whether that difference is meaningful for patients. Long-term data were lacking, and wavelength, dose, session length and number of sessions varied widely between studies.

Here, red light is added to hands-on treatment and exercise, not used in place of them. It is one tool inside a plan, never the whole plan.

### How Dr. Ed uses it

Dr. Ed applies red light to the treated area, usually after manual therapy, for about 10 minutes as part of the visit. It is not a separate treatment and is not sold on its own. It is best avoided on skin with eczema or an infection. His device is a KOZE LED unit (KOZE lists 660 and 850 nanometers, red and near-infrared, for its panels), not a laser, so read the studies above as a guide to the field rather than a promise about this device.

# Electrical stimulation (TENS and NMES) in Chatsworth physical therapy

Two kinds of electrical stimulation: TENS for pain, NMES to make a weak muscle fire.

### TENS: for pain

TENS stands for transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation. Dr. Ed puts pads, called electrodes, on your skin and connects them to the TENS unit. The unit sends a gentle current through the pads, and the aim is to calm the pain.

Dr. Ed uses TENS for back pain, neck pain and pain after surgery.

### NMES: for a weak muscle

NMES stands for neuromuscular electrical stimulation. In physical therapy it does a different job from TENS: a current through pads on your skin makes a weak muscle fire.

Dr. Ed uses NMES when a muscle will not switch on after surgery (see [post-op physical therapy](/pre-post-surgical-rehabilitation/)) or after a stroke (see [neurological rehabilitation](/neurological-rehabilitation/)). One example is the quad, the large muscle on the front of your thigh, after knee surgery (see [knee replacement recovery](/conditions/knee-replacement-recovery/)).

### How it fits into a visit

Electrical stimulation in physical therapy is one part of your plan, not all of it. At the Chatsworth clinic, Dr. Ed starts with an evaluation. Tell Dr. Ed about any implanted device, such as a pacemaker, before your first visit.

Dr. Ed uses stimulation here at the clinic. He does not set patients up with a TENS unit for home use, though he can give you some basic guidance if you already use one.

### What Dr. Ed does about it

Frozen shoulder, also called adhesive capsulitis, moves through three stages ([AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/diseases--conditions/frozen-shoulder) describes them this way). Dr. Ed shows you which one you are in.

- **Freezing.** The shoulder hurts more and more and begins to lose movement.
- **Frozen.** The pain may ease, but the stiffness stays.
- **Thawing.** Movement slowly improves.

Frozen shoulder treatment with Dr. Ed is hands-on, and the aim is to get the shoulder moving. The work includes:

- **End-range mobility work** at the point where the shoulder stops, to win back a little more movement.
- **Joint mobilization**, where Dr. Ed's hands move the joint through small, controlled motions.
- **External rotation** work, turning the arm outward a little further as the shoulder allows.
- **Cuff and scapular strengthening** for the rotator cuff and the muscles that move the shoulder blade.
- **A home program** to keep the work going between visits.

Frozen shoulder is slow, and Dr. Ed says so plainly. Therapy shortens the stiff and thawing stages.

You start with an evaluation. Dr. Ed examines how your shoulder moves, finds what is limiting it and explains it in plain terms. You work with him one-on-one.

This is not a one-visit fix. The pace depends on the stage your shoulder is in and how it responds, so Dr. Ed does not quote a number of visits up front.

Evening and Saturday appointments are available, so treatment can fit around work.

## P-038 Referral for physical therapy in California (/blog/referral-for-physical-therapy-in-california/)

Meta title: Referral for Physical Therapy in California? | Dr. Ed, DPT
Meta description: You can start physical therapy in California without a referral for 45 days or 12 visits. Dr. Ed Shut, DPT explains the two rules and the Medicare rule.

# Do you need a referral for physical therapy in California? No, with two rules

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Insurance and cost](/insurance-and-cost/).

No, California does not require a referral to start physical therapy: [Business and Professions Code section 2620.1](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5-7/article-2/section-2620-1/) lets you keep going without one for up to 45 calendar days or 12 visits, whichever comes first. After that, you need a referral from your doctor to continue, and Medicare patients need a referral from the start.

### The two rules: 45 calendar days and 12 visits

Starting physical therapy without a referral from your doctor is called direct access. California allows it, with two limits set out in California Business and Professions Code section 2620.1 ([read it on Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5-7/article-2/section-2620-1/)).

- **Limit one: 45 calendar days.** Every day on the calendar counts, weekends included.
- **Limit two: 12 visits.** This one counts appointments, not days.
- **Whichever comes first.** Come often and you reach 12 visits before day 45. Come less often and day 45 arrives before your twelfth visit. The earlier one is your limit.

The count does not reset on its own. After 12 visits you need a referral to keep going, and if a new problem starts after you finish, you need a referral to be seen through insurance, especially with Medicare.

Until you reach that limit, you can book and start treatment without a referral. You do not have to see your doctor first.

### What happens at the limit

At the limit, treatment can go on only with your doctor's approval of the physical therapist's plan of care. The clinic calls this a referral. Under [section 2620.1](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5-7/article-2/section-2620-1/), the approval is a dated signature on the plan of care, from a physician and surgeon or a podiatrist.

[Section 2620.1](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5-7/article-2/section-2620-1/) also says the approval must include an in-person or telehealth examination and evaluation of your condition, and testing if it is indicated. In plain terms, getting the referral means an appointment with your doctor.

Dr. Ed does not guess at the number of visits up front. It depends on your condition and how your body responds, so he sets the plan after your first evaluation. Since treatment cannot continue past the limit without the approval, it helps to see your doctor before you reach it.

[Image: Dr. Ed explaining an anatomy chart to a patient]

### Medicare is the exception

Medicare patients need a referral from the start, so the 45-day and 12-visit window does not apply. [Medicare's page on physical therapy services](https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/physical-therapy-services) says your doctor or other health care provider "must certify that you need it."

If you have Medicare, ask your doctor for the referral and bring it to your first visit.

### What the law says, and how Dr. Ed puts it

The limits come from California Business and Professions Code section 2620.1 ([read it on Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5-7/article-2/section-2620-1/)). The key passage says the physical therapist "shall not continue treating the patient beyond 45 calendar days or 12 visits, whichever occurs first" without receiving "a dated signature on the physical therapist's plan of care indicating approval of the physical therapist's plan of care."

The same section sets a rule for any point in treatment. If the physical therapist has reason to believe you have signs or symptoms of a condition that needs treatment beyond a physical therapist's scope of practice, or that you are not progressing toward documented treatment goals as shown by objective, measurable, or functional improvement, the physical therapist "shall refer the patient to a person holding a physician and surgeon's certificate" or to a dentist, podiatrist or chiropractor.

Direct access does not widen what a physical therapist may do. The section "does not expand or modify the scope of practice for physical therapists set forth in Section 2620, including the prohibition on a physical therapist diagnosing a disease."

Nor does the section require your health plan to cover direct access. Subdivision (d) says nothing in the section "shall be construed to require a health care service plan, insurer, workers' compensation insurance plan, employer, or state program to provide coverage for direct access to treatment by a physical therapist." Whether your plan pays without a referral is a question for the plan.

Here is how Dr. Ed states the rule at the clinic:

"California law lets you see a physical therapist without a referral for up to 12 visits or 45 days, whichever comes first. After that, we need a referral from your doctor to continue. Medicare patients need a referral from the start."

### What to do next

If you are not on Medicare, you can book now without a referral. If you are on Medicare, get the referral from your doctor first. The [Insurance and cost](/insurance-and-cost/) page has the details on insurance and payment. If you are not sure where you stand, say so on the form below.

The 45 calendar day and 12 visit limit, approval of the plan of care, referral out, scope of practice and coverage.

# A fall-prevention checklist for your parent's house

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Balance and fall prevention](/balance-and-fall-prevention/).

Go through your parent's house with the CDC's home fall checklist: keep objects off the stairs and floors, stop rugs from slipping, light the stairs, put a lamp by the bed, and add a non-slip mat and grab bars in the bathroom. Then book a balance evaluation with Dr. Ed Shut, DPT, in Chatsworth, because the checklist fixes the house and his clinic tests show how your parent stands, turns and gets up from a chair.

### Walk the house with the CDC checklist

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) publishes a home fall checklist for older adults, [Check for Safety: A Home Fall Prevention Checklist for Older Adults](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/59197/cdc_59197_DS1.pdf). It is part of the CDC's STEADI materials, and its stated purpose is to help you find and fix hazards in the home.

The list below is a short version. Every item comes from that brochure, which has more questions and fixes, so read the whole thing too. Go through the house one area at a time, and walk it with your parent if you can.

**Stairs**

- Always keep objects off the stairs.
- Have an electrician put in an overhead light and light switch at the top and bottom of the stairs.
- Make sure handrails are on both sides of the stairs, and are as long as the stairs.
- Make sure the carpet is firmly attached to every step, or remove the carpet and attach non-slip rubber treads to the stairs.

**Floors**

- Remove the rugs, or use double-sided tape or a non-slip backing so the rugs won't slip.
- Pick up things that are on the floor. Always keep objects off the floor.
- Coil or tape cords and wires next to the wall so your parent can't trip over them.
- Ask someone to move the furniture so your parent's path is clear.

**Shelves and step stools**

- Keep the things your parent uses often on the lower shelves (about waist high).
- If your parent must use a step stool, get one with a bar to hold on to. Never use a chair as a step stool.

**Bedrooms**

- Place a lamp close to the bed where it's easy to reach.
- Put in a nightlight so your parent can see where they're walking.

**Bathrooms**

- Put a non-slip rubber mat or self-stick strips on the floor of the tub or shower.
- Have grab bars put in next to and inside the tub, and next to the toilet.

Three fixes need another person: an electrician for the stair lights, someone to move the furniture, and someone to put in the grab bars.

The checklist covers the house. It cannot show you how steady your parent is on their feet.

- Gait training: stride, turning, stairs, curbs and uneven ground.

# When a young gymnast's back pain is not just a growing pain

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Pediatric physical therapy](/pediatric-physical-therapy/).

Back pain in a young gymnast is worth an evaluation when it lasts more than two weeks, comes with a limp or swelling, wakes your child at night, or shows up at the same point in practice every time. Any one of those signs is reason enough to book, and Dr. Ed will tell you straight if it is nothing to worry about.

### Why a growing gymnast's back hurts differently

Growing bodies get hurt differently. Growth plates are softer than the tendons that pull on them, so a teenager's lower back can hurt in ways an adult's would not. A flexible young gymnast or dancer can also be strong in one direction and unprotected in another.

Rady Children's Health says teenage gymnasts and football linemen are at higher risk of stress fractures in the lower spine that cause symptoms. It points to the repeated hyperextension of the lower back in their sports, which means arching backward again and again ([Rady Children's Health](https://www.rchsd.org/health-safety/growing-up-columns/linemen-gymnasts-at-highest-risk-of-spondylolysis/)).

### Five signs that point past a growing pain

These are the five signs to watch for. The same list applies to a young dancer.

- Pain that lasts more than two weeks.
- A limp.
- Swelling.
- Pain that wakes your child at night.
- Pain that shows up every time at the same point in practice.

None of these proves an injury. Each one is a reason to have it looked at.

### One cause worth knowing about: spondylolysis

That kind of stress fracture is called spondylolysis. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes it as a weakness or stress fracture in one of the vertebrae, the small bones that make up the spinal column ([OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/spondylolysis-and-spondylolisthesis)). Rady Children's Health says it is seen more often in young football linemen, gymnasts, dancers and martial artists ([Rady Children's Health](https://www.rchsd.org/health-safety/growing-up-columns/linemen-gymnasts-at-highest-risk-of-spondylolysis/)).

OrthoInfo says the most common symptom is lower back pain, which may feel similar to a muscle strain and may get worse with activity and better with rest ([OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/spondylolysis-and-spondylolisthesis)). Rady Children's Health describes the main symptom as pain in the lower back, especially when bending backward ([Rady Children's Health](https://www.rchsd.org/health-safety/growing-up-columns/linemen-gymnasts-at-highest-risk-of-spondylolysis/)).

A blog post cannot tell you whether your child has it. OrthoInfo says your child's doctor examines the back and spine, and that imaging tests help confirm the diagnosis ([OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/spondylolysis-and-spondylolisthesis)). # Knee replacement recovery: what the first six weeks should look like

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Knee replacement recovery](/conditions/knee-replacement-recovery/).

In the first six weeks after a knee replacement, the work is to keep the swelling down, get the knee straightening and bending, walk with less help and wake the thigh muscles back up. Your surgeon's protocol sets the rules, and physical therapy works through them in stages, with a written home program that changes as the knee heals.

### Your surgeon sets the rules

Bring your surgeon's post-op protocol and your discharge paperwork to the first visit. Dr. Ed works from your protocol, not from a generic plan.

Physical therapy starts when your surgeon says so. On Dr. Ed's [pre and post-op physical therapy page](/pre-post-surgical-rehabilitation/), that is within the first one to two weeks for most joint replacements. The timing points below come from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS). They are general ranges, and where your surgeon says something different, your surgeon wins.

### The first days at home

Before you leave the hospital, AAOS says you need to meet several goals, including walking with an assistive device (a cane, walker or crutches) on a level surface, climbing up and down two or three stairs, and doing the prescribed home exercises ([Activities After Knee Replacement, AAOS](https://www.orthoinfo.org/recovery/activities-after-knee-replacement/)).

If you go straight home, AAOS says you will need some help at home for several days to several weeks, so arrange help before surgery. It says swelling can be moderate to severe in the first few days or weeks and mild to moderate for about 3 to 6 months, and that you reduce it by raising your leg and applying ice.

Driving and work have their own timing. AAOS says that in most cases it is safe to drive again once you are no longer taking opioid pain medication and your strength and reflexes have returned to a more normal state, and that your doctor will help you decide when. Going back to work can take several days to several weeks, depending on your job and your recovery.

AAOS also lists warning signs of infection and of a blood clot, such as persistent fever, drainage from the wound, pain in the leg or calf unrelated to your incision, increasing swelling of the calf, ankle or foot, and shortness of breath or sudden chest pain. - **At first.** AAOS says you will walk with a walker or crutches.
- **Often about 2 to 3 weeks.** AAOS says you can begin using a single crutch or cane when you can walk and stand for more than 10 minutes and your knee is strong enough that you are not carrying any weight on the walker or crutches.
- **Usually 4 to 6 weeks.** AAOS says you can repeat the exercises with light weights around your ankle.

The exercises also outlast the six weeks: AAOS says to keep doing the ones your physical therapist prescribes for at least 2 months after surgery ([Activities After Knee Replacement, AAOS](https://www.orthoinfo.org/recovery/activities-after-knee-replacement/)). ## P-045 Vertigo and BPPV: why the room spins (/blog/vertigo-bppv-why-the-room-spins/)

Meta title: Vertigo and BPPV, Explained by a Physical Therapist | Dr. Ed
Meta description: Learn why the room spins when you roll over, how a physical therapist tests for BPPV and why the repositioning maneuver often works in a few visits.

# Vertigo and BPPV: why the room spins when you roll over, and what a physical therapist does about it

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Vertigo and BPPV](/conditions/vertigo-bppv/).

One common reason the room spins when you roll over is BPPV, or benign paroxysmal positional vertigo: small crystals in your inner ear break off and move into a canal, and when you move your head a certain way they stimulate nerve endings and make you dizzy, according to the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) in its [guide to BPPV](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo-bppv). A physical therapist can test for it with positional tests that recreate your symptoms, then manage most cases with maneuvers that move the crystals back where they belong, as the same guide explains.

### Why the room spins when you roll over

[APTA's guide](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo-bppv) calls BPPV a common inner ear problem that affects the vestibular system, the system that works to maintain your balance. Benign means the disorder is not life-threatening. Paroxysmal means the spinning comes on suddenly. Positional means a change in head position sets it off, such as lying down, turning over in bed or looking up.

According to APTA, the inner ear holds a layer of calcium carbonate material called otoconia, and pieces of it are often called crystals. In BPPV, those crystals break off and move into a part of the inner ear called the semicircular canal. When you move your head a certain way, they shift inside the canal, stimulate nerve endings and make you dizzy. Most often the cause is not known, though a head injury, an infection or aging can loosen the crystals.

Each spell is brief and intense, usually about 15 to 45 seconds according to APTA. Episodes frequently occur for weeks or months at a time. You may feel like the room is spinning around you, and you may also feel light-headed, off-balance or nauseous. APTA also says the sensation can be alarming and may increase the risk of falling.

Get medical help right away if dizziness comes with symptoms such as weakness, slurred speech or vision problems. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001420.htm) says these may be signs of a more serious condition.

### How a physical therapist tests for BPPV

[APTA says](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo-bppv) physical therapists use positional tests to recreate BPPV symptoms. The tests require moving your head in specific ways while the therapist watches your eyes for specific movements.

[MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001420.htm) says a provider may diagnose BPPV with a test called the Dix-Hallpike maneuver. The provider holds your head in a certain position, you lie quickly backward over a table, and the provider looks for abnormal eye movements (called nystagmus) and asks if you feel like you are spinning.

APTA notes that dizziness has many causes and is often hard to describe, so it says to be as specific as possible and to mention:

- how long your symptoms last
- what makes them better or worse
- any recent illnesses or injuries
- problems with your immune system
- changes in medications or hormones
- whether you have headaches

Those clues help the therapist make an accurate diagnosis, or show that you need a referral to another specialist.

### What the repositioning maneuvers do

[APTA says](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo-bppv) the majority of people with BPPV can be treated with simple, specific maneuvers that involve moving the head and body. The therapist evaluates you first and decides which maneuver is right for you. The maneuvers are designed to move the crystals from the semicircular canal back into the appropriate area of the inner ear, called the utricle.

[MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001420.htm) names the Epley maneuver and describes it as a series of head movements to reposition the canaliths, the small pieces of calcium, in your inner ear. It also says BPPV is uncomfortable but can usually be treated with that maneuver.

BPPV can come back. APTA says there are no known ways to prevent it, and that within three years of a bout, symptoms return for about half of people. MedlinePlus says the procedure may need to be repeated if symptoms come back.

Dr. Ed Shut, DPT, does the positional test and the repositioning maneuvers for BPPV at Chatsworth Physical Therapy. He is trained in the Dix-Hallpike test and the Epley maneuver, and he screens you first to make sure they are right for you. For the most common kind of vertigo, BPPV, repositioning maneuvers done in the clinic often settle it within a few visits. Other dizziness improves with a graded exercise program.

He sets the plan after your first evaluation rather than guessing up front. How many visits you need depends on your condition and how your body responds. Every visit is one-on-one with Dr. Ed.

## P-046 Return to sport after ACL surgery (/blog/return-to-sport-after-acl-surgery/)

Meta title: Return to Sport After ACL Surgery: The Tests | Dr. Ed, DPT
Meta description: Pain gone is not the same as ready to play. Dr. Ed Shut, DPT explains the hop, cutting and strength tests an athlete passes before returning after ACL.

# Return to sport after ACL surgery: the tests you pass before you play

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [ACL recovery](/conditions/acl-recovery/).

Before you play again after ACL surgery, pass strength, single-leg hop and cutting tests; the pain stopping or the months going by is not enough on its own. Dr. Ed runs the rehab in stages and retests you against what your sport demands before you go full speed.

### Why a pain-free knee is not a ready knee

Pain going away is not the same as being ready to play. The injury that comes back is usually the one that was rested, felt fine and was never retested. So Dr. Ed does not go by how the knee feels, or by the calendar alone. He retests you.

The research points the same way. In a two-year study of 106 people who played pivoting sports after ACL reconstruction, the re-injury rate fell by 51% for each month return to sport was delayed until nine months after surgery, with no further drop after that ([Grindem and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/13/804)). More even strength in the quadriceps, the muscle on the front of the thigh, between the two legs before returning also lowered the re-injury rate.

That was one study of one group, so it is not a forecast for you. It does show that both time and thigh strength count. Dr. Ed tests each stage before the next one starts.

### The tests, in plain terms

The studies behind this post tested strength, hops and running. The strength and hop tests set the leg that had surgery against your other leg.

- **Strength.** How hard the quadriceps, the muscle on the front of your thigh, can push, set against the other leg.
- **Hops.** Hopping on one leg for distance and for time, set against the other leg.
- **Cutting.** Planting your foot and changing direction at speed. Dr. Ed tests it if your sport calls for it.

Each test needs a pass mark. The Delaware-Oslo study used four single-leg hops: a single hop for distance, a triple hop for distance, a crossover hop for distance and a 6 m timed hop. It set the pass mark at above 90 on every test, and advised against full jumping, pivoting and hard-cutting sport until strength and hopping were back above 90% of the uninjured leg ([Grindem and colleagues](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/13/804)).

A second study followed 158 male professional athletes after ACL reconstruction. Before going back, they took a battery of strength, running and hop tests. Those who returned to team training without meeting all six discharge criteria had about four times the risk of tearing the graft ([Kyritsis and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/15/946)). All were professional men, so read it as a direction, not a forecast.

[Image: Ankle treatment during a sports injury rehab session in Chatsworth]

### How Dr. Ed stages it

Dr. Ed works from your surgeon's protocol, not from a generic plan, and runs rehab in stages:

1. Calm the injury.
2. Restore range.
3. Rebuild strength.
4. Retest against what your sport demands: the single-leg hop, cutting, overhead load and backbends, whichever your sport asks for.

You know where you are at every visit and what the next gate is, the test you pass to move on. ACL recovery is a nine-to-twelve-month road for an athlete, done in stages and tested before each one. How long yours takes depends on the surgery and your surgeon's timeline, and Dr. Ed gives you a realistic answer at the evaluation and updates it as you progress.

Passing is not a promise that the knee will never be hurt again. It means the knee has met the demands of your sport in the clinic before it meets them in a game.

An ACL patient's own account is on the [Reviews](/reviews/) page.

### Protecting the other knee

For athletes who have had an ACL injury, the same testing becomes a prevention program for the other side.

The numbers show why. A review of studies of younger athletes after ACL reconstruction found a second ACL injury in 15% overall: 7% in the knee that had surgery and 8% in the other knee. For athletes younger than 25 who returned to sport, the rate was 23% ([Wiggins and colleagues, American Journal of Sports Medicine](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0363546515621554)).

### What to do next

Ask whoever clears you to play which tests you passed and by how much. For ACL rehab at Chatsworth Physical Therapy, see [ACL recovery](/conditions/acl-recovery/).

## P-047 Red light therapy for back and knee pain (/blog/red-light-therapy-back-knee-pain/)

Meta title: Red Light Therapy for Back and Knee Pain | Dr. Ed Shut, DPT
Meta description: See what red light therapy can do for back and knee pain inside a physical therapy plan, what the evidence says and what it cannot replace. By Dr. Ed.

# Red light therapy for back and knee pain: what it does, and what it does not do

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Red light therapy](/red-light-therapy/).

Red light therapy may ease back or knee pain for some people in the short term, but the research is mixed, and a 2018 Washington State review rated the overall quality of the evidence as low or very low. It is not a cure: at Dr. Ed's clinic it is an add-on inside a plan of hands-on work and exercise, never the plan itself.

### Does red light therapy work?

The studies below test low-level laser therapy (LLLT), a treatment that applies low-intensity laser light at a specific wavelength. A Washington State Department of Labor and Industries report ([read the report](https://www.lni.wa.gov/patient-care/_docs/HTAupdateLLLT_011818.pdf)) notes that "Laser wavelength, energy density, treatment duration, numbers of sessions and site of application are highly variable." So read the findings as a guide to the field, not a promise about one machine.

A 2017 meta-analysis, which pools many trials, covered 18 studies and 1,462 adults with musculoskeletal disorders ([abstract on PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145397/)). The pooled difference in pain between laser and control groups was -0.85 (95% CI: -1.22 to -0.48), meaning less pain with laser. Its authors say it presents evidence that LLLT is an effective treatment modality to reduce pain, and that following dose recommendations "seems to enhance treatment effectiveness." They also report high heterogeneity (I2 of 85.6%): the trials disagreed with each other a lot.

The Washington report was more guarded. Four of its conclusions, word for word:

- "Overall, the quality of the evidence for LLLT was low or very low."
- "The results on efficacy are highly inconsistent."
- "There seems to be a small statistically significant difference between LLLT and placebo for some indications in short term, but the clinical meaningfulness is uncertain."
- "Long term data are lacking."

### Red light therapy for back pain

A 2015 meta-analysis of nonspecific chronic low back pain included seven trials with 394 patients ([abstract in Arthritis Research & Therapy](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13075-015-0882-0)). Pooling five of them, pain after treatment was lower with laser than with placebo. The authors write that LLLT "is an effective method for relieving pain" but that "there is still a lack of evidence supporting its effect on function." Disability and spinal range of motion showed no significant effect.

An older Cochrane review, summarized in the Washington report, found "statistically significant but clinically unimportant pain relief" in three small studies. It concluded that "there are insufficient data to draw firm conclusions on the clinical effect of LLLT for low-back pain."

### Red light therapy for knee pain

The knee studies here cover osteoarthritis. A 2019 meta-analysis in BMJ Open ([read the paper](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/9/10/e031142)) pooled 22 placebo-controlled trials with 1,063 participants. At the end of therapy, laser lowered pain by 14.23 mm more than placebo on a 100 mm pain scale, and disability was also reduced. Its authors note that major knee osteoarthritis guidelines do not recommend LLLT.

The drop was larger, 18.71 mm, at the doses the authors classed as recommended. The 2019 review covers knee osteoarthritis only, not other causes of knee pain. A Hayes review summarized in the Washington report suggested that laser above a certain dose can bring immediate relief of pain and disability in knee osteoarthritis, but that the effect is temporary. It called the evidence low-quality and inconsistent.

### What red light therapy does not do

- **It is not a cure.** Less pain after treatment is not the same as fixing the cause, and the Washington report finds long term data lacking.
- **It does not replace hands-on work and exercise.** Dr. Ed's back and knee plans rest on hands-on treatment, strengthening and a short home program. Red light is added to them.
- **It is not a sure thing.** The trials disagree, and the Washington report calls the short-term gain over placebo small.

### How Dr. Ed uses red light

Dr. Ed starts by examining how you move and working out what is causing the pain. If red light fits, he adds it where he judges it may help a tissue recover or settle a muscle spasm, alongside hands-on treatment, strengthening and a home program.

[Image: Red light therapy session at Chatsworth Physical Therapy]

Dr. Ed's red light protocol: about 10 minutes on the treated area, inside a visit, never on its own and not at every visit. His device is a KOZE LED unit (KOZE lists 660 and 850 nanometers, red and near-infrared, for its panels), not a laser. At any clinic, ask what the light is meant to do, how many minutes and how often, and how you will tell whether it is helping.

### What to do next

Start with the cause, not the light. Book an evaluation and Dr. Ed will explain what is behind your pain and whether [red light therapy](/red-light-therapy/) has a place in your plan.

2. [Technology Assessment Update: Low Level Laser Therapy for Musculoskeletal Disorders](https://www.lni.wa.gov/patient-care/_docs/HTAupdateLLLT_011818.pdf), Washington State Department of Labor and Industries, 2018, read 21 Oct 2026

## P-048 Sciatica: disc or hip? (/blog/sciatica-disc-or-hip/)

Meta title: Sciatica: Disc or Hip? How the Evaluation Tells | Dr. Ed
Meta description: Leg pain called sciatica can start at the disc or at a tight hip muscle, and the treatment differs. Dr. Ed Shut, DPT explains how the evaluation tells.

# Sciatica: is it the disc or the hip? How a physical therapy evaluation tells

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Sciatica](/conditions/sciatica/).

Sciatica is leg pain from an irritated nerve, and the irritation usually comes from a disc or from a tight muscle in the hip. A physical therapy evaluation tells which: Dr. Ed watches how you stand, bend, twist, sit and walk, tests the hips and checks whether a nerve is involved, because the cause is often somewhere other than where it hurts.

### What the word sciatica means

Sciatica describes where the pain travels, not what is causing it. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000686.htm) says sciatica "refers to pain, weakness, numbness, or tingling in the leg" and is caused by injury to or pressure on the sciatic nerve. It calls sciatica a symptom of a medical problem, not a medical condition by itself. The same page says the nerve starts in the lower back and runs down the back of each leg.

The [American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons](https://orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/sciatica/) (AAOS) calls it "a very broad term describing nerve pain, not a specific diagnosis." It describes the pain as felt in the lower back or hip and spreading to the back of the thigh and into the leg.

### Two places the nerve can be irritated

Dr. Ed's treatment aims at what is irritating the nerve, usually a disc or a tight muscle in the hip, and at the movements that keep irritating it. Which one it is shapes the plan.

On the disc side, the AAOS says sciatic pain occurs "when the nerve root in the lumbar spine is compressed (squeezed)," and names a herniated disc as one cause of that compression. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507908/), a medical reference on the National Library of Medicine Bookshelf, calls a herniated or bulging lumbar disc the most common cause of sciatica.

On the hip side, [the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/piriformis-syndrome) describes piriformis syndrome. It happens when the piriformis, a thin muscle in the buttocks that allows the hip to rotate, presses against the sciatic nerve. StatPearls says that, because the sciatic nerve is so close, any injury or inflammation of that muscle can cause sciatica symptoms. Sciatica that starts at a tight hip muscle is a specialty of Dr. Ed, and he sees it often.

These are not the only causes. MedlinePlus also lists spinal stenosis and pelvic injury or fracture among the common ones. So the word sciatica does not tell you which cause is behind it.

### How the evaluation tells them apart

Dr. Ed does not start with the sore spot. This is the sequence, in order:

1. He watches how you stand, bend, twist, sit and walk.
2. He tests the hips, the mid-back and the muscles that are supposed to be holding the spine steady.
3. He checks whether a nerve is involved.
4. He explains what he found in plain terms and tells you what the plan is.

[Image: Dr. Ed guiding a patient through a core stability exercise for back pain at Chatsworth Physical Therapy]

Two steps speak to the question in the title: he tests the hips, and he checks whether a nerve is involved. The cause is often somewhere other than where it hurts. The evaluation is where he finds it, at a disc, in a tight hip muscle or somewhere else.

For comparison, StatPearls calls sciatica a clinical diagnosis, so a thorough history and physical examination are necessary for a complete evaluation and diagnosis. The AAOS says a physical examination may help pinpoint the irritated nerve root. It adds that a doctor may ask you to squat and rise, walk on your heels and toes, or perform a straight-leg raising test or other tests. MedlinePlus adds that tests such as imaging and blood tests are often not needed unless pain is severe or long-lasting.

### When to contact a doctor right away

MedlinePlus lists signs that mean you should contact your health care provider right away. They include:

- Weakness or numbness in your buttocks, thigh, leg, or pelvis
- Loss of control of urine or stool
- Back pain after a severe blow or fall
- Unexplained fever with back pain
- Severe pain and you cannot get comfortable

It also says to contact your provider if this episode of back pain has lasted longer than 4 weeks. The full list is on the MedlinePlus page.

### What to do next

You do not need to arrive knowing whether it is the disc or the hip. That is what the evaluation is for. Read more about [sciatica](/conditions/sciatica/), see what patients say on the [Reviews](/reviews/) page, or book an evaluation below.

## P-049 Cash-pay physical therapy cost (/blog/cash-pay-physical-therapy-chatsworth-cost/)

# Cash-pay physical therapy in Chatsworth: what a visit costs and how a superbill works

By Dr. Edward Shut, DPT. 2026-10-21. Filed under [Insurance and cost](/insurance-and-cost/).

Cash-pay means you pay for the visit yourself, and if you have a PPO plan the clinic gives you a superbill, an itemized receipt you send to your insurer for reimbursement. Direct-pay visits are $175 for the first evaluation and $125 for each follow-up visit.

### What cash-pay physical therapy means

Cash-pay, also called direct pay, means you pay for your own visits. Direct-pay patients are welcome at Chatsworth Physical Therapy. How it works depends on your situation.

- **No insurance.** Physical therapy without insurance means you pay for each visit yourself: $175 for the first evaluation, then $125 a visit.
- **A PPO plan.** You pay at the visit, then send your insurer a superbill and ask for reimbursement.
- **Medicare or a personal injury case.** These work differently from cash-pay. The last section says where to start.

### What a visit costs

The first evaluation is $175. Each follow-up visit is $125.

The evaluation is a full first visit: Dr. Ed checks how you walk and move, tests your mobility, finds the pain points and confirms your pain is mechanical, the kind physical therapy treats. If it is not, he tells you and refers you to the right doctor.

A visit runs about 60 minutes, depending on what you need, and every one is one-on-one with Dr. Ed, from your first evaluation through discharge.

How many visits you need depends on your condition and how your body responds. Dr. Ed sets the plan after your first evaluation rather than guessing up front, so he does not quote a number of visits before he has seen you.

### How a superbill works

A superbill is an itemized receipt you send to your insurer for reimbursement. Itemized means it lists each service you paid for. The clinic provides the superbill, you send it, and your plan decides what it pays back.

[Image: Dr. Edward Shut, DPT, at his desk in the Chatsworth Physical Therapy clinic]

#### A worked example

Say you have a PPO plan and you book a first visit. The steps leave out amounts on purpose, because the amounts depend on the clinic's rate and on your plan.

1. **Ask your insurer first.** Find out whether your plan reimburses physical therapy that you pay for yourself, and how it wants a superbill sent.
2. **Come in for the visit.** The first one is an evaluation with Dr. Ed.
3. **Pay at the visit.** You pay the clinic yourself.
4. **Get your superbill.** This is the itemized receipt you will send in.
5. **Send it to your insurer.** Use the method your insurer gave you in the first step.
6. **Your plan decides what it pays back.** Only your insurer can tell you the amount.

### What your insurer decides

Four terms from the HealthCare.gov glossary will help when you call your insurer.

- **PPO plan.** The [HealthCare.gov glossary](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/preferred-provider-organization-ppo/) defines a PPO as a type of health plan that contracts with providers to create a network. It adds: "You pay less if you use providers that belong to the plan's network. You can use doctors, hospitals, and providers outside of the network for an additional cost."
- **Non-preferred provider.** The [HealthCare.gov glossary](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/non-preferred-provider/) defines it this way: "A provider who doesn't have a contract with your health insurer or plan to provide services to you." It adds: "You'll pay more to see a non-preferred provider."
- **Out-of-network coinsurance.** The [HealthCare.gov glossary](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/out-of-network-coinsurance/) defines it as the percentage of the allowed amount that you pay, for covered health care services, to providers who do not contract with your health insurance or plan. It adds: "Out-of-network coinsurance usually costs you more than in-network coinsurance."
- **Allowed amount.** The [HealthCare.gov glossary](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/allowed-amount/) defines it this way: "The maximum amount a plan will pay for a covered health care service." It adds: "If your provider charges more than the plan's allowed amount, you may have to pay the difference."

These are general definitions. They do not tell you what your plan will pay back for a superbill. Only your insurer can tell you that, so ask it about each of these before your first visit:

- Whether your plan reimburses physical therapy when you pay the provider yourself and send in a superbill.
- What the plan needs with the superbill, and where to send it.
- Whether there is a deadline for sending it in.
- What share of the cost you would pay yourself.
- Whether it wants a referral or approval from your doctor first.

### What to do next

With the rates on this page in hand, put the questions above to your insurer. For Medicare, a personal injury case or any other way to pay, start with [insurance and cost](/insurance-and-cost/). When you are ready, book below.
